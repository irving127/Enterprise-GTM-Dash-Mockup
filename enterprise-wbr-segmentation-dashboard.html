Foundation · Know what you sell

Enterprise AI makes money four ways, and each one forecasts differently

Before any number in this deck makes sense, the revenue streams have to be separated. An AI business is not a CRM business: the pricing unit, the revenue timing and the risk are different in every stream, so a WBR that blends them produces one confident, wrong number. The segments on the next slides map to these streams. In this deck’s worked example, streams 1 and 2 are the main story: usage and the commitments that draw down against it. Streams 3 and 4 are the two delivery constraints that decide whether the first two actually land.

STREAM 1 · USAGE API

LLM API usage, paid per token

Customers pay for what they consume: input tokens, output tokens and cached input are priced separately on published rate cards, and reasoning-heavy models cost more per call. There is no licence event: revenue is the sum of millions of API calls, so it compounds with adoption and drops the day usage drops.

Forecast it from usage curves (tokens per day, per workload), never from signatures.
STREAM 2 · COMMITMENTS

Enterprise commitments with drawdown

The customer signs an annual (or multi-year) spend commitment and draws it down as usage accrues. Usage above the commit bills as overage; at term end there is a true-up. The catch this whole deck returns to: signing is not revenue. Revenue recognises as the customer actually consumes, and consumption ramps weeks or months behind the signature.

Track two numbers: bookings (signed) and consumption (drawn down). They are never the same in a given quarter.
STREAM 3 · FDE & SERVICES

Forward-deployed engineering

Paid proofs of concept and proofs of value, deployment projects, and production go-live work, sometimes standalone, sometimes bundled into a commitment. This stream is sold in engineer-hours, which makes it the only stream with a hard physical ceiling: when the FDEs are booked out, this revenue stops, however strong demand is.

Forecast it from capacity (slide 8), not from pipeline.
STREAM 4 · SELF-HOSTED

On-prem and private deployment

Open-weight models deployed in the customer’s own cloud or data centre, charged as a platform or licence fee plus deployment services. Regulated buyers choose this stream precisely because data never leaves their boundary, which means data-residency approvals and security reviews, not price, decide the timing.

Forecast it from gates cleared, not from stages: a deal behind a residency gate has a binary, not a probability.
Say

“Four streams, four forecasting methods. Usage from curves, commitments from drawdown, services from capacity, self-hosted from gates. Most AI forecast errors are a method applied to the wrong stream.”

Ask the room

Which of the four streams is your number actually made of, and does your current forecast treat it that way?

Lesson 2 · Segmentation

Enterprise AI is not one business. A self-hosted open-weight deployment in Tokyo and a usage-based API account in Sydney differ in pricing unit (licence vs. tokens), revenue timing, security gate and forecast behaviour. Lesson 3 · Forecast language

Coverage, commit and forecast are three different promises

Most forecast fights are vocabulary fights. Half the room means “what’s possible”, the other half means “what I’ll be fired for missing”. Name the three numbers and never let one do another’s job.

01 · COVERAGE

What could happen

Open pipeline divided by what is left to book. A health metric, not a promise. Below roughly 2× at mid-quarter, no heroic close rate saves you.

This quarter: 2.4× overall, 1.8× in the self-hosted segment.
02 · COMMIT

Bookings we will be held to

The signed number the team stakes its name on: commitments, licences and services with a named buyer, a dated next step and no unresolved external gate. In AI, commit is a bookings promise, not a revenue one.

This quarter: $41.2M of bookings, 86% of target.
03 · FORECAST

Two numbers, always

AI needs a bookings forecast (what will sign) and a consumption forecast (what customers will actually use and we can recognise). They diverge because usage ramps after signing: a commitment signed in week 10 may draw down mostly next quarter.

This quarter: bookings $45.2M, recognised revenue $38.4M.

Where the numbers sit against target

The tick marks the $48.0M bookings target. The lowest bar is not bad news, it is physics: recognised revenue trails bookings while API usage and commitment drawdown ramp.

Lesson 5 · Post-mortems

Every forecast method has a known bias. Post-mortem it, or repeat it

Take the questions, leave the dashboard

The dashboard was never the point. If these three questions change how your next weekly review runs, the quarter did its teaching job.

Q1

Can we say the verdict in one sentence?

If not, the review is collecting data instead of forming a judgement. Start next week’s meeting by drafting the sentence together.

Q2

Reflection · The valuable part

What I learned, coming from outside enterprise

My background is ads sales strategy and operations, not enterprise. Building this review is how I bootstrapped my understanding of enterprise AI GTM, and these five lessons are the part worth sharing: each one is a misunderstanding I had to correct, stated so the next person can skip it. This slide is written to be lifted straight into the repo README; the five headings are the short version.

LEARNING 1

Signing is not revenue

The challenge

My first forecast treated signed enterprise commitments as revenue for the quarter they signed in.

I misunderstood

That a signature starts the revenue. In usage pricing, a signature starts a ramp: the customer still has to migrate workloads and consume.

What I learned

Bookings and consumption are two different forecasts. In this deck’s example: $45.2M of bookings vs. $38.4M recognised, and both numbers are honest.

Fix / check

Ask “signed, or consumed?” about every number, and track commitment drawdown weekly, not signatures.

LEARNING 2

Coverage without conversion is false comfort

The challenge

A blended 2.4× coverage figure made the quarter look safe in aggregate.

I misunderstood

That pipeline volume is safety. Volume multiplied by a conversion rate you have not checked is just a bigger guess.

What I learned

Per segment, the story flipped: self-hosted sat at 1.8× coverage behind a binary residency gate, hidden inside the healthy blend.

Fix / check

Never quote coverage without the segment’s conversion rate next to it, and never blend gated segments into an average.

LEARNING 3

FDE hours are inventory, not a tap

The challenge

When PoC and PoV demand spiked, my instinct was that engineering would flex to meet it.

I misunderstood

FDE capacity as something you turn on. It is roughly 30 deployable hours per engineer per week, and new engineers take weeks to onboard and ramp.

What I learned

FDE hours behave like inventory with a lead time (slide 8): you forecast demand, hold stock, and reorder before you run out.

Fix / check

Run the FDE supply-vs-demand check every WBR, and start onboarding when projected demand passes 90% of supply, not 100%.

LEARNING 4

Sales forecasts run rosy early in the quarter

The challenge

Early-quarter sales calls kept coming in high, then revised down week by week (last quarter: $49.5M called in week 3, $44.9M actual).

I misunderstood

At first I read the pattern as sandbagging in reverse, or as a data problem.

What I learned

It is incentives plus genuine uncertainty: early optimism costs nothing. The earlier the week, the more evidence a number needs behind it.

Fix / check

Compare the sales call against the conversion-implied number weekly (slide 7); when they differ by more than 10%, ask which assumption is wrong.

LEARNING 5

Deployment mode changes how revenue is recognised

The challenge

I forecast API deals and self-hosted deals with the same stage probabilities, because both were “late-stage enterprise deals”.

I misunderstood

That a deal is a deal. An API deal’s revenue follows a usage curve after signing; a self-hosted deal’s revenue follows gates: residency approval, security review, in-boundary deployment.

What I learned

Gates are binary, not probabilistic. A deal behind an uncleared gate is not 70% likely; it is 0% until the gate opens, then it moves.

Fix / check

Forecast each stream with its own method: usage from curves, commitments from drawdown, services from capacity, self-hosted from gates cleared.

THE META-LESSON

Enterprise GTM is mostly translation

The challenge

Coming from ads, the vocabulary itself was the barrier: commit, coverage, drawdown, PoV, true-up.

I misunderstood

That the jargon was decoration. Each term turned out to encode a real mechanical difference in how money moves.

What I learned

The fastest way to learn enterprise GTM is to rebuild one quarter’s numbers yourself and keep asking what has to be true for each number to be real.

Fix / check

If you are crossing over from another GTM motion, steal this deck’s structure and swap in your own segment numbers.

Questions this dashboard does not answer

Every number in this walkthrough has a definition, a source, or a CTA. These five do not, and that is deliberate: they are the questions a dashboard can raise but never settle. Pick one and argue about it. That argument is worth more than another chart.

Q1

What does “good coverage” actually mean when conversion is unproven?

3× pipeline made of deals that never close is 0× coverage wearing a costume. Is coverage a property of the pipeline, or of the evidence inside it?

Q2

Who owns a forecast when sales and operations disagree?

The person who calls the number, the person measured on it, or the room that heard both cases? And does the answer change who is allowed to be wrong?

Q3

When should one strong proof of value outweigh a pipeline of maybes?

A single PoV with a real buyer and real data is evidence. Twenty stage-3 deals are arithmetic. How much proof should it take to move a forecast?

Q4

What are we optimising for beyond this quarter?

Revenue now, logos that become references, deployments that teach the product team something? A WBR that only sees 13 weeks will reliably sacrifice all three of the others.

Q5

If the number stopped mattering for one week, what would this meeting talk about?

Whatever surfaces is probably what the review is actually for: coordination, honesty, or control. The dashboard cannot tell you which one your team needs most.

Q6

Whose voice is missing from this data entirely?

The customer appears here as pipeline, gates and slips. What would this quarter look like told from their side of the table, and which of our seven lessons would survive the retelling?
